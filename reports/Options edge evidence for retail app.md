# Option edges live in volatility, not direction

Credible research finds durable options edges almost entirely in how volatility is priced, not in forecasting stock direction. Most of those edges have either shrunk since publication or are eaten by the spreads retail traders pay. The S&P 500 variance risk premium behind the "sell index vol" story was large before about 2012: implied vol ran 2.5 to 5.6 points above realized, and long variance swaps lost more than 50% a month in log terms over 1996–2003. Since about 2012, however, standard index option-selling strategies show no statistically significant alpha. Since 2007, Cboe's put-write index has a lower Sharpe ratio than the S&P 500 (0.52 vs 0.61), and short-vol blowups in 2018 and 2020 wiped out leveraged sellers. Single-stock implied volatility is not overpriced on average. The single-stock edge is cross-sectional, led by the Goyal-Saretto realized-minus-implied sort, which replicates at mid-quotes with t-statistics of 9 to 20 through 2019–2020 but loses about two-thirds of its return once traders pay 50–75% of the quoted spread. Option-implied predictors of stock returns (skew, call-put IV spread, IV changes, option volume) were strong in 1996–2011 data, but they have since faded. The smirk is now insignificant at a one-month horizon, the call-put spread has roughly halved, and a 2025 *Journal of Financial Economics* paper shows that at least two-thirds of their predictability sits in hard-to-borrow stocks. No published study supports classic daily-bar stock signals as directional option trades. What they do affect is volatility pricing: options on recent winners are relatively cheap and options on losers relatively rich, worth roughly 0.4–0.7% a month after full spreads in delta-hedged form. Buyers of 0DTE (same-day expiry) options lose on average, and the sellers' same-day premium (about 0.01% of spot per day) does not survive corrected trading costs. A retail round trip costs about 4% of premium at the money, 14% slightly out of the money and 28% deep out of the money. For an app that generates direction from daily bars, the evidence supports using options data to choose and filter the instrument rather than as a new source of alpha. The options-derived signals most worth testing on 2020–2026 end-of-day data are the implied-minus-realized vol gap, the IV term slope, the call-put IV spread, one-month IV changes, risk-neutral skewness and option momentum.

*Status labels in the tables: **Replicated** means it holds in independent or later data. **Weakened** means it is smaller in later data. **Decayed/failed** means it is insignificant or reversed in later data. **Contested** means credible counter-evidence exists. **Unverified** means the figure comes from a secondary summary, preprint or draft that was not checked against the final text.*

## The index variance premium was real, correlation-driven, and has faded since 2012

Before about 2012, S&P 500 options were reliably priced above the volatility that followed. In Carr and Wu's 1996–2003 sample, the 30-day synthetic variance-swap rate (a VIX-style measure) averaged 24.41 vol points, ATM implied vol 21.28, and subsequent realized vol 18.82. Implied therefore exceeded realized by **about 5.6 points on the VIX-style measure but only about 2.5 points on ATM implied vol**. The log return on a long variance swap averaged worse than −50% per month (t = −9.48), and shorting S&P and Dow variance swaps gave raw annualized information ratios above 3 ([Carr & Wu working paper](https://engineering.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/2019-01/CarrReviewofFinStudiesMarch2009-a.pdf); [RFS 2009 record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v22y2009i3p1311-1341..html)). Driessen, Maenhout and Vilkov (DMV) find a similar **2.4-point gap** over 1996–2012 (model-free implied 23.09% vs realized 20.69%) ([DMV 2013](https://www.netspar.nl/wp-content/uploads/061_Driessen.pdf)). A Cboe-sponsored study puts average VIX at 19.3 against realized 15.1 for 1990–2018 ([Cboe summary of Bondarenko](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/white-paper-shows-volatility-risk-premium-facilitated-higher-risk-adjusted-returns-for-put-index/)). Strategy returns tell the same story. Zero-beta ATM index straddles lost about **3% per week** ([Coval & Shumway 2001](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/361566)). Delta-hedged long S&P 500 options underperformed zero ([Bakshi & Kapadia 2003](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v16y2003i2p527-566.html)). In Bondarenko's working paper, one-month ATM puts on S&P 500 futures returned about **−39% per month** over 1987–2000 ([CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-options/is-40-per-month-shorting-index-puts-a-fair-return); [QJF 2014](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wsi/qjfxxx/v04y2014i03ns2010139214500153.html)).

The gap between the VIX-style and ATM figures matters for any app. The VIX weights out-of-the-money puts by 1/K², so with normal put skew it typically sits at **1.1–1.2 times ATM implied vol** ([Cboe Insights](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/how-can-long-volatility-go-down-when-the-vix-index-goes-up)). In the Carr-Wu numbers, about 3.1 of the 5.6 points was a skew and convexity premium that an ATM straddle seller never collects. An app that compares ATM implied vol with realized vol should therefore expect an edge roughly half the size of the popular "VIX minus realized" figures. The Cboe 4.2-point number compares VIX with an average of noisy short-window realized vols, which biases realized vol downward, so it probably overstates the harvestable gap further.

The Nasdaq-100 premium was much weaker. Over 1996–2003, NDX ATM implied vol exceeded realized by only about 0.65 points, and the arithmetic variance premium was statistically insignificant (t = −0.94 for NDX, −1.82 for QQQ) in a sample dominated by the dot-com boom and bust ([Carr & Wu](https://engineering.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/2019-01/CarrReviewofFinStudiesMarch2009-a.pdf)). No credible recent NDX/QQQ estimate exists.

Single-stock options are not systematically overpriced the way index options were. Carr and Wu find the arithmetic premium significant for only 3 of 35 stocks. DMV find average constituent implied vol (43.38%) statistically indistinguishable from realized (41.99%), and they cannot reject a zero premium for 503 of 919 S&P 500 stocks ([DMV 2013](https://www.netspar.nl/wp-content/uploads/061_Driessen.pdf)). The index premium was instead a **correlation premium**: index options priced average correlation at 39.46% against 32.59% realized. DMV conclude that this premium "cannot be exploited with realistic trading frictions" ([DMV 2009, JF](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v64y2009i3p1377-1406.html)).

The premium then faded. Dew-Becker and Giglio's Chicago Fed working paper (1987–2025, 457 monthly observations) finds a **structural break around 2012**. After it, 5%-OTM puts, ATM straddles and variance swaps "no longer have statistically significant alphas or information ratios." The straddle buyer's Sharpe ratio moved from −0.74 in 1987–2001 to **+0.12 after 2012**. Measured over rolling 10-year windows, the VIX's average has been close to the average of realized vol for about 17 years ([Dew-Becker & Giglio, June 2026 draft](https://www.dew-becker.org/documents/option_decline.pdf); [Chicago Fed WP 2025-17](https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/working-papers/2025/2025-17)). They link the decline to dealers' net S&P gamma turning neutral around August 2012 and to a wider set of investors, retail included, becoming option suppliers. Bates (2022), as they cite it, dates the decline nearer 2017, so the exact break is uncertain.

Heston and Todorov's tradable variance portfolios (January 2006 to October 2020) show the same thing from another angle. The S&P 500 premium averaged −22.9% per month excluding March 2020 but only −11.9% including it, and over the full period it was **not significantly different from zero** ([Heston & Todorov](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2024/program/paper/hiTeT8SE)).

Cboe's own benchmark depends on the window. From 1986 to 2015, the PUT index earned 10.1% a year with a 0.67 Sharpe ratio, against 9.8% and 0.47 for the S&P 500 ([CXO summary of Bondarenko 2016](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-options/performance-of-cboe-putwrite-indexes)). Since January 2007 it has earned **7.1% a year with a 0.52 Sharpe, against 11.0% and 0.61 for the S&P 500 total return**, and it beat the index in only two of the eleven calendar years from 2015 to 2025 ([Cboe PUT factsheet](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/indices/factsheet/CboeGlobalIndices_PUT-Index.pdf)). Since 2007, put writing has behaved like a lower-beta (0.6) equity holding rather than a source of alpha.

Short volatility's losses concentrate in a handful of days, and leverage decides who survives them. On 5 February 2018, short-VIX exchange-traded products lost more than 90% in a single day through a rebalancing feedback loop ([Augustin, Cheng & Van den Bergen, FAJ 2021](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2021/volmageddon-failure-short-volatility-products)). LJM Partners, a manager of more than $1 billion in margined short S&P futures options, lost more than 80% over 5–6 February and closed, and regulators later charged it with misstating worst-case risk ([CFTC](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/8392-21)). In March 2020 the S&P 500 variance portfolio returned **more than 2,000% in one month**, enough by itself to roughly halve the 2006–2020 average premium ([Heston & Todorov](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2024/program/paper/hiTeT8SE)). Allianz Global Investors' Structured Alpha options program also lost billions that month. One crash-scenario loss shown to an investor had been altered from −42.15% to −4.15%, and the case ended with more than $1 billion paid to the SEC and over $5 billion in restitution ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2022-84)).

Unlevered, cash-secured put writing did better: its maximum drawdown from 2007 was −32.7%, against −50.9% for the S&P 500. It still lost more than the index in 2018 (−5.9% vs −4.4%) and badly lagged the 2020 rebound (+2.1% vs +18.4%) ([Cboe PUT factsheet](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/indices/factsheet/CboeGlobalIndices_PUT-Index.pdf)). Its monthly return skewness over 1986–2018 was −2.09, against −0.81 for the index ([Cboe/Bondarenko](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/white-paper-shows-volatility-risk-premium-facilitated-higher-risk-adjusted-returns-for-put-index/)).

Whether sellers are fairly paid for crash risk is still disputed. Bondarenko argues that index puts are too expensive for any standard model. Broadie, Chernov and Johannes counter that put-writing returns are statistically consistent with standard models once sampling error and jump-risk premia are counted ([LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/32229)).

## Realized-minus-implied sorts replicate at mid-quotes but lose most of their return to spreads

The single-stock edge comes from sorting stocks against each other, not from a blanket "sell single-stock vol" premium. Goyal and Saretto sort stocks on historical minus ATM implied volatility. They buy straddles or delta-hedged options where implied vol is low relative to realized vol and sell them where it is high. The long-short portfolio "produces an economically and statistically significant average monthly return" that standard risk factors do not explain ([Goyal & Saretto 2009, JFE](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v94y2009i2p310-326.html)).

At mid-quotes the returns are very large. Saretto's 2005 precursor forecast IV changes partly from IV relative to realized vol and to its own 12-month average, and earned **12.9% per month** long-short on straddles with a monthly Sharpe ratio of 0.98 over 1996 to June 2005 ([Saretto 2005](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/CrossOptions.pdf)). An independent replication through June 2019 finds a **10.34% monthly quintile spread (t = −9.48)** ([Heston et al. working paper](https://www.acem.sjtu.edu.cn/sffs/2021w/pdfs/3.pdf)). The effect has not failed to replicate.

Trading costs do the damage. Charging 75% of the quoted spread cut Saretto's hold-to-expiry return from 16.1% to **5.5% per month**. Goyal and Saretto's 2006 draft fell from 15.0% to **5.3%** at half the quoted spread ([Saretto 2005](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/CrossOptions.pdf); [CXO summary of 2006 draft](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/volatility-effects/making-money-with-options-based-on-superior-volatility-forecasts)). Muravyev and Pearson report that the published strategy's 22.7% monthly return falls to **3.9%** at quoted spreads ([Muravyev & Pearson 2015 WP](https://c.mql5.com/forextsd/forum/209/Option%20Trading%20Costs%20Are%20Lower%20than%20You%20Think.pdf)). A thesis summary reports that Do, Foster and Gray (2016) questioned whether the trade survives costs at all; the original could not be checked ([University of Vaasa record](https://osuva.uwasa.fi/handle/11111/6167)).

The original authors' 2022 Dallas Fed paper reinterprets what is left. Over 1996–2020 the realized-minus-implied sort still earns 3.5–3.6% per month (t ≈ 20). A latent-factor model with time-varying factor exposures (IPCA), however, cuts its alpha to **1.0% per month (t = 9.4)**. Of 44 option characteristics, 29 survive multiple-testing correction, but only 5 of those keep significant alphas under IPCA. The authors conclude that the evidence presents "no significant challenges to market efficiency" ([Goyal & Saretto 2022](https://www.dallasfed.org/-/media/documents/research/papers/2022/wp2214.pdf)).

Two further features matter for retail use. First, most of the profit comes from the short side. In the 2019 replication, even the cheap-IV quintile lost 1.86% per month on straddles, against −12.20% for the rich-IV quintile ([Heston et al.](https://www.acem.sjtu.edu.cn/sffs/2021w/pdfs/3.pdf)). Capturing the spread means selling volatility. Second, holding to expiry lowers costs but concentrates jump exposure.

More than a dozen other characteristics predict delta-hedged returns, and most relate to how costly the option is for dealers to hedge.
- **Idiosyncratic volatility.** Delta-hedged returns fall as the stock's idiosyncratic volatility rises. The long-short spread was about 2% per month at mid in 1996–2009 but "approximately zero" after paying 50–100% of the closing spread ([Cao & Han 2013](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/cross-section-of-option-returns-and-idiosyncratic-stock-volatilit/); [CXO summary of WP](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/volatility-effects/extracting-a-volatility-premium-with-equity-options)). It is only marginal in the 2019 replication (−2.49%, t = −1.99).
- **IV term-structure slope.** This is stronger, at **7.17% per month (t = 7.92)** for straddles through 2019 ([Vasquez 2017, JFQA](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v52y2017i06p2727-2754_00.html); [Heston et al.](https://www.acem.sjtu.edu.cn/sffs/2021w/pdfs/3.pdf)).
- **Fundamentals.** Zhan, Han, Cao and Tong show that profitability, cash holdings, share issuance and analyst dispersion predict returns to writing delta-hedged calls, with annual Sharpe ratios above 2 that remain profitable after costs ([Zhan et al., RFS 2022](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/option-return-predictability/)). In their 1996–2012 draft, seven of eight sorts stay significant when the full quoted spread is paid. Size is the exception: 3.79% at mid, −0.22% at full spread ([Zhan et al. 2016 WP](https://abfer.org/media/abfer-events-2016/annual-conference/investment-finance/226-option-return-predictability.pdf)).
- **Volatility of volatility.** It predicts returns negatively ([Cao, Vasquez, Xiao & Zhan 2023](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/30606/)).
- **Machine learning.** A combination of 265 characteristics over 12 million delta-hedged option returns (1996–2020) earns "statistically and economically sizable profits even after accounting for transaction costs" ([Bali et al., RFS 2023](https://www.wiwi.uni-muenster.de/fcm/en/publication/98329575)).

The richest options belong to small, illiquid, high-volatility names, and that is also where spreads and short-vol tail risk are largest. Rankings by gross return and by net return therefore diverge.

The best-documented "momentum" in options is in the options' own past returns. Straddles with high returns over the previous 2–12 months beat those with low returns by **6.22% per month (t = 7.97)** over 1996–2019, with no long-run reversal. One-month straddle returns reverse (−4.09%, t = −6.18) ([Heston et al., JF 2023](https://www.econbiz.de/Record/option-momentum-heston-steven/10014437686)). The authors' cost table shows how fragile this is. The baseline momentum spread drops to −0.40% when 52% of the quoted half-spread is paid and to −6.78% at the full quoted spread. Only a cost-optimized composite of momentum and reversal survives: it is restricted to extreme deciles and to options with spreads under 10% of mid. That composite keeps **3.48% per month (t = 2.59) at 76% effective spreads** but an insignificant 1.98% at full quoted spreads. Adding the cost of liquidating stock received on exercise pushes the full-spread figure to −0.30% ([Heston et al.](https://www.acem.sjtu.edu.cn/sffs/2021w/pdfs/3.pdf)).

Muravyev and Pearson show that traders who time their executions pay effective spreads under 40% of conventional measures ([Muravyev & Pearson 2020, RFS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v33y2020i11p4973-5014..html)). That is why these strategies stay profitable under "algo" cost assumptions (about 20% of the quoted half-spread) and fail for retail market orders.

No published study tests post-publication decay of single-stock option anomalies, and no peer-reviewed sample extends these signals past 2020. The samples end close to publication (Goyal-Saretto 2009, Cao-Han 2013, Vasquez 2017, Zhan et al. 2022, Heston et al. 2023, Bali et al. 2023), so 2020–2026 data is genuinely out of sample. Two signs point to smaller live returns: dynamic-risk models explain most of the alpha, and the profit sits on a short-volatility leg whose index-level premium has collapsed since 2012.

## Option-implied stock predictors have halved, died, or turned out to be borrow-fee proxies

Steep put skew was the most celebrated option-implied stock predictor, and it is the clearest case of decay. Xing, Zhang and Zhao found that stocks with the steepest smirk (OTM put IV minus ATM call IV) underperformed the flattest by **10.9% a year, risk-adjusted**, over 1996–2005. The predictability lasted about six months, and the steep-smirk firms had the worst earnings surprises the following quarter ([Xing, Zhang & Zhao 2010, JFQA](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v45y2010i03p641-662_00.html)). The original statistical evidence was only moderate. The Open Source Asset Pricing replication project records a t-statistic of 2.19 for the weekly long-short portfolio, rates the result "likely" rather than "clear," and notes that it is sensitive to data screens ([OSAP signal documentation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/master/SignalDoc.csv)). A related smile-slope measure, put minus call IV at ±0.50 delta (Yan 2011), showed 1.8% per month with t = 8.17 in the same era ([OSAP](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/master/SignalDoc.csv)).

In recent data, an unreviewed 2026 arXiv preprint uses end-of-day OPRA data for 2015–2026. It finds the one-month smirk coefficient shrinking by about three-quarters, from t = −5.5 in 2015–2019 to an **insignificant t = −1.5 in 2023–2026**, with a wrong-signed three-month coefficient in the latest period ([Li & Wang 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26115); [full text](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26115v1)). The preprint's arXiv identifier and its stated submission date do not match, so its provenance needs checking, but its direction agrees with everything else in this section.

The call-put IV spread has survived best. Cremers and Weinbaum found that stocks with relatively expensive calls, compared with matched puts, beat stocks with relatively expensive puts by **50 basis points per week** over 1996–2005, on both the long and short sides. They also found that predictability declined within their own sample ([Cremers & Weinbaum 2010, JFQA](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v45y2010i02p335-367_00.html)). Bali and Hovakimian's ATM call-minus-put IV spread earned **1.045% per month, value-weighted (t = 4.2)**, over 1996–2004 ([Bali & Hovakimian 2009](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.1090.1063); [OSAP](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/master/SignalDoc.csv)). Later evidence shows decay but not death. A Copenhagen Business School master's thesis finds 19 bp per week (t = 2.45) over 2011–2015, about 40% of the original ([CBS thesis](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/60755923/306868_Trading_on_deviations_from_put_call_parity_and_mean_reversion.pdf)). The 2026 preprint's one-month coefficient roughly halves from 2015–2019 (t = 6.6) to 2023–2026 (t = 4.2) but stays significant ([Li & Wang 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26115v1)). One European study found the strategy unprofitable ([AUEB record](https://pyxida.aueb.gr/items/492b4004-31e3-49cf-b5a8-9f7a040f986d)).

Changes in implied volatility and option volume tell a similar story. An, Ang, Bali and Cakici show that large one-month increases in call IV predict higher next-month stock returns and large increases in put IV predict lower returns. Decile spreads were about **1% per month**, lasting up to six months, over 1996–2011 ([An et al. 2014, JF](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2008902)). The combined change (ΔPut IV − ΔCall IV) is strongest, at 1.68% per month (t = 6.77) equal-weighted. The put-only signal is weak (t = 2.03) ([OSAP](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/master/SignalDoc.csv)). No post-2011 test exists.

Pan and Poteshman's put-call ratio predicts more than **40 bp the next day and more than 1% the next week**. It is built only from volume where buyers open new positions, in proprietary CBOE data, and the authors attribute its power to non-public information rather than market inefficiency ([Pan & Poteshman, NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10925)). A public end-of-day put-call ratio is not the same signal. The publicly computable option-to-stock volume ratio earned about **0.34% per week** for the low-minus-high decile over 1996–2010, and its predictability was stronger where short-sale costs were high ([Johnson & So 2012, JFE](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v106y2012i2p262-286.html)). Ge, Lin and Pearson find that the information comes mainly from opening call purchases, which suggests informed traders use calls for leverage ([Ge, Lin & Pearson 2016](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v120y2016i3p601-622.html)). A Korean study finds the opposite sign ([JDQS](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/JDQS-11-2023-0036)).

The realized-minus-implied spread used as a stock-return predictor was weak to begin with: **0.732% per month, t = 2.9**, over 1996–2004 ([OSAP](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OpenSourceAP/CrossSection/master/SignalDoc.csv)). A 2024 EFMA paper reports that it no longer holds, though its full text could not be verified ([EFMA 2024](http://www.efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2024-Lisbon/papers/ATR_RVolIVOl_Complete.pdf)). At the market level, Bollerslev, Tauchen and Zhou found that the index variance premium predicts quarterly equity returns, with R² above 15% over 1990–2005 ([Federal Reserve FEDS 2007-11](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2007/200711/index.html)). Heston and Todorov find the opposite for most of 20 futures markets over 2006–2020 ([Heston & Todorov](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2024/program/paper/hiTeT8SE)).

The most important recent finding concerns mechanism. Muravyev, Pearson and Pollet show that the IV spread mechanically embeds the stock's borrow fee. **Predictability from the IV spread and skew falls by at least two-thirds when high-borrow-fee stocks are excluded**, and the pattern is similar for other option-implied predictors ([Muravyev, Pearson & Pollet 2025, JFE](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v172y2025ics0304405x25001618.html)). Only about 10% of stocks carry borrow fees of 2% or more, and retail platforms rarely show the fee ([Gies College of Business](https://giesbusiness.illinois.edu/news/2026/01/07/study--borrowing-fee-is-the-variable-that-debunks-theory-that-options-predict-future-stock-returns)). In a companion *Journal of Finance* paper, the option-implied lending fee predicts returns, and once it is included the IV spread and skew no longer do ([Muravyev profile](https://giesbusiness.illinois.edu/profile/dmitriy-muravyev)). This contradicts Cremers and Weinbaum's original claim that short-sale constraints did not drive their result.

The effects also concentrate where stock liquidity is low and option liquidity is high ([Cremers & Weinbaum](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v45y2010i02p335-367_00.html)). Several headline numbers (Yan's smile slope, An et al., the smirk, the option-to-stock volume ratio) are equal-weighted, so they tilt toward small stocks. Two signals have held up in recent data. The preprint's risk-neutral skewness proxy stays strongly negative in every regime from 2015 to 2026 (t between −10.5 and −12.2). A boosted-tree model beat linear models out of sample only in 2023–2026 (R² 1.29% vs 0.07%) ([Li & Wang 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26115v1)).

All of the IV-based signals were built from end-of-day OptionMetrics data and tested at weekly to monthly horizons, so end-of-day option chains are enough to reproduce them. The volume signals with the largest effects need signed, open/close-classified volume that is not in standard public data. Where effects survive in recent data, they are shifting toward shorter horizons. In 2023–2026, next-day and next-week smirk coefficients remain significant while the monthly effect has gone ([Li & Wang 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26115v1)). Shorter horizons raise turnover and cost exposure, and any next-day result from end-of-day data risks picking up stale closing quotes rather than information.

## Stock momentum and reversal predict option volatility pricing, not profitable directional bets

No peer-reviewed study tests whether a stock's distance from its 200-day (or 50-day) moving average predicts option returns. None shows that buying calls on momentum winners or puts on losers earns abnormal option returns. What the literature shows is that past stock returns move the volatility component of option prices.

Zhan and coauthors find that delta-hedged option gains rise with both the 12-minus-2-month return and the prior one-month return. In their 1996–2012 draft, the long-short spread from writing delta-hedged calls was **1.58% per month at mid for momentum and 1.28% for short-term reversal**. Paying the full quoted spread cut these to **0.42% (t = 2.89) and 0.73% (t = 5.13)** ([Zhan et al. 2016 WP](https://abfer.org/media/abfer-events-2016/annual-conference/investment-finance/226-option-return-predictability.pdf)). Two details stand out. Both signals point the same way for options even though they point opposite ways for stocks. And in that sample of optionable stocks, none of the eight anomalies that predicted option returns, momentum and reversal included, significantly predicted the *stock* returns themselves.

An, Ang, Bali and Cakici add that stocks with high past-month returns see call IV rise over the next month while realized volatility falls ([An et al. 2014](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19590.pdf)). The contrary evidence comes from Heston and coauthors. Over 1996–2019 they find a slightly negative, insignificant link between past stock returns and straddle returns, and no relation between stock momentum and option momentum ([Heston et al.](https://www.acem.sjtu.edu.cn/sffs/2021w/pdfs/3.pdf)). Saretto also finds that the momentum factor plays little role in explaining volatility-sorted straddle returns ([Saretto 2005](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/CrossOptions.pdf)).

Read together, these results suggest a structure-selection rule rather than an edge. On a hedged basis, options on recent winners have been relatively cheap and options on recent losers relatively rich. A momentum signal (buy winners) is therefore better matched to buying calls, where the directional bet and the volatility tilt agree. A short-term reversal signal (buy recent losers) is better matched to selling puts or put spreads, which collect the rich volatility while expressing the same bullish view. This is an inference from delta-hedged, cross-sectional effects of roughly 0.4–0.7% per month after full spreads in 1996–2012 data. It has not been tested on directional trades or after 2012, and short puts on falling stocks carry jump risk.

The case against simple directional option buying is stronger. The average single-stock ATM straddle lost **5.3% per month** over 1996–2019, and even the top option-momentum quintile lost 1.55% per month ([Heston et al.](https://www.acem.sjtu.edu.cn/sffs/2021w/pdfs/3.pdf)). Retail buy portfolios lose money even before trading costs ([Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13285)).

A rough calculation shows how high the hurdle is. Take a one-month ATM call on a stock with 30% implied vol; its premium is about 3.5% of the stock price. Delta-hedged calls lost about **4.9% of option price** by expiry in Cao and Han's 1996–2009 sample ([CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/volatility-effects/extracting-a-volatility-premium-with-equity-options)). A retail ATM round trip costs about 4.2% of premium at typical effective spreads, or 8.7% at full quoted spreads (see the frictions section below). Together, the volatility cost and the round trip come to roughly 9–14% of premium, or 0.3–0.5% of the stock price. With a delta near 0.5, **the stock must beat its expected return by roughly 0.6–1.0% within the month** just to break even. That is a demanding bar for signals whose stock-level predictive power among optionable names was insignificant in 1996–2012.

The only "distance from average" evidence in options is in volatility, not price. A stock whose ATM implied vol sits below the cross-sectional average and below its own 12-month moving average tends to see implied vol rise the next month, and vice versa ([Saretto 2005](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~mkearns/finread/CrossOptions.pdf)). The testable question for a 200-day trend signal is whether option prices already reflect it. The IV-minus-realized and term-slope measures can detect that.

Daily-bar signals used for 0DTE trades have no support. The closest direct test is Vilkov's out-of-sample rules for 10:00 ET entries. They showed net Sharpe ratios near 1 until an August 2026 correction found that bid-ask costs had been undercharged by a factor of 100. After the correction the put-ratio rule fell to **−0.75** and the top-three basket to **−0.82** ([Vilkov KNOWN-ISSUES log](https://github.com/vilkovgr/0dte-strategies/blob/main/KNOWN-ISSUES.md)).

## 0DTE buyers lose on average and sellers' same-day premium vanishes after corrected costs

Same-day options now dominate index trading. SPX 0DTE contracts were **59% of SPX volume in 2025** (2.3 million contracts a day) ([Markets Media](https://www.marketsmedia.com/cboe-reports-record-trading-volume-for-2025/)), up from about 5% in 2016 ([BIS Quarterly Review, March 2024](https://www.bis.org/publications/what-could-explain-recent-drop-vix)). Daily expirations exist for SPX, XSP, SPY, QQQ and IWM. Single stocks do not have daily expirations. In January 2026 the SEC approved Monday and Wednesday expirations for seven mega-cap names plus Broadcom and XLF on Nasdaq ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/sec-approves-expanded-option-expirations-113650138.html)). Whether those have launched is unconfirmed.

Every major academic study finds that buying 0DTE SPX options loses money on average. Dim, Eraker and Vilkov (2012 to June 2023) find negative average returns for ATM calls, puts and straddles held to the close. Annualized, the 0DTE variance premium is about **five times** that of 11–22-day options. In daily terms it is **only about 0.01%** of the index, "hardly profitable given the required delta-hedging intensity... and realistic transaction costs" ([Dim, Eraker & Vilkov 2024](https://westernfinance-portal.org/viewpaper?n=950096)). Almeida, Freire and Hizmeri (2012 to March 2025) find an annualized 0DTE premium of 1.80 percentage points at a 10:00 entry rising to 2.96 at 14:00, against 0.56 for 1-day options, driven mainly by upside variance. Their strategy of trading options that violate price bounds was "highly profitable, even after transaction costs" until 2022, and the profit dissipated once daily expirations arrived ([Almeida et al. 2025](https://www.fma.org/assets/docs/Derivatives2025/Almeida.pdf)).

The seller side looked better until a correction. Vilkov's "0DTE Trading Rules" (September 2016 to February 2026) originally reported positive net Sharpe ratios for several structures. In August 2026 his replication repository disclosed that the half-spread (about 2.2 bp of the index) had been charged at 1/100 of its true size. After the fix, the put ratio spread went from +0.84 to **−0.61**, the bear put spread from +0.30 to −0.73, and the iron butterfly/condor from −0.96 to **−2.67**. Of the structures reported, only the risk reversal stayed positive, at a negligible +0.10. The log concludes "No structure retains a materially positive net Sharpe ratio" ([Vilkov KNOWN-ISSUES log](https://github.com/vilkovgr/0dte-strategies/blob/main/KNOWN-ISSUES.md)). The correction supersedes the March 2026 SSRN abstract, which still describes "economically meaningful net performance" ([SSRN 4641356](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4641356)). Before the correction, the worst 1% of days already cost short structures **0.58–1.58% of the index** ([annotated paper](https://github.com/vilkovgr/0dte-strategies/blob/main/docs/paper/paper-annotated.md)). A 2026 HKUST study (2020–2024) similarly finds that costs and estimation risk make volatility-arbitrage Sharpe ratios economically insignificant, though only a summary was available ([HKUST Business Insight](https://bm.hkust.edu.hk/bizinsight/2026/04/are-0dte-options-mispriced)).

Retail outcomes are negative, but how large the losses are is disputed. Beckmeyer, Branger and Gayda identify retail by trades flagged in Cboe's price-improvement auction. Over February 2021 to September 2023, retail lost **more than $125 million** in SPX 0DTE, more than $90 million of it in transaction costs. That is $241,000 a day overall and $350,000 a day after daily expirations began in May 2022. Single-leg positions accounted for more than $100 million of the losses. Multi-leg positions earned 0.17% more than single-leg ones, and retail put and call spreads had positive median margin-adjusted returns ([Beckmeyer et al., Dec 2023](https://wp.lancs.ac.uk/fofi2024/files/2024/04/FoFI-2024-146-Leander-Gayda.pdf)). Bogousslavsky and Muravyev use trader-level broker data for 2020–2022. They find **93.3% of retail 0DTE trades start with a purchase** and lose 4.6% per trade, while naked option sales earn +20% ([Bogousslavsky & Muravyev 2024](https://www.lsu.edu/business/files/event-files/2025-finance-mardi-gras/retail_option_trading_v2.pdf)).

There is counter-evidence. SEC economists find that customer limit orders resting at the best bid or offer fill cheaply. All-in cost was $0.021–0.028 per contract, against $0.05 for marketable orders, and an OPRA timestamp sequencing problem inflates some loss estimates ([Fu, Li, Musto & Pearson, SEC DERA 2025](https://www.sec.gov/files/dera-hope-reasonable-prc-2503.pdf)). A Cboe-funded paper finds Cboe customer price-improvement trades profitable on average over 2020–2023. It argues that the auction proxy captures only 4–6% of SPX trading, an implausibly low share for retail ([Amaya et al. 2025](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/education/research_publications/Retail_Profitability.pdf)). Estimates of retail's share of SPX 0DTE range from about 6% under that proxy to **50–60%** in Cboe's own estimates ([Cboe, May 2025](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/0-dt-es-decoded-positioning-trends-and-market-impact)), which are commercially interested.

Timing within the day matters. In 2004–2013, delta-hedged SPX options lost about 1% from close to open and gained 0.3% from open to close ([Muravyev & Ni 2020, JFE](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v136y2020i1p219-238.html)). That pattern made overnight holding the expensive leg for option buyers. Modern 0DTE studies still find a positive intraday premium, so the older intraday long-vol profit does not carry over. Cboe's VIX1D index rises during the day and falls overnight for methodological reasons, and it overestimates realized volatility ([Albers & Kestner 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finlet/v62y2024ipas1544612324002162.html); [Albers 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jfutmk/v45y2025i11p2092-2108.html)). Any signal that compares VIX1D with realized vol needs to adjust for that.

## Retail round trips cost 4% of premium at the money and 28% deep out of the money

The best measurements of retail option costs come from Bryzgalova, Pavlova and Sikorskaya's study of single-leg, price-improved retail trades across all US equity and ETF options exchanges from November 2019 to June 2021 ([Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya, JF 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13285); [open-access copy](https://lbsresearch.london.edu/id/eprint/2827)). The effective spread is defined as twice the distance between the trade price and the midpoint. Two marketable orders, one to open and one to close, therefore cost roughly one effective spread of premium.

Moneyness and ticket size matter far more than product type. Retail trades are 72% ATM and 24% slightly OTM by volume, and 42% of retail trades are under $250 ([BPS 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13285)). In S&P 500 constituents over 1996–2007, short-dated OTM calls had quoted spreads of 34% of mid, against 23% for long-dated OTM calls ([CXO summary of Christoffersen et al.](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=13300)). Muravyev and Pearson cite average quoted spreads of 17.2% for options on S&P 500 stocks, against 3.55 bp for the stocks themselves ([BPS 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13285)). In SPX 0DTE, retail effective spreads were reported at 6.1% for calls and 4.9% for puts, about half the non-retail level, though this comes from a press summary ([WealthManagement.com](https://www.wealthmanagement.com/equities/retail-investors-are-in-love-with-same-day-options)).

Price improvement roughly halves the quoted spread for retail marketable orders, but routing for payment for order flow (PFOF) leaves money on the table. Ernst and Spatt find that when the exchange's designated market maker pays PFOF, a $2 option fills about **2 cents per share (1%) worse**, while the broker collects only 0.4 cents. Applied to $770 billion of auction trades over 26 months, that implies **more than $7 billion** of added retail cost, against $2–3 billion of total options PFOF ([Ernst & Spatt, NBER w29883](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29883/revisions/w29883.rev0.pdf)). Traders who time their executions pay under 40% of conventional effective spreads, but "retail and other investors who are not able to time executions" pay the full conventional effective spread ([Muravyev & Pearson 2015 WP](https://c.mql5.com/forextsd/forum/209/Option%20Trading%20Costs%20Are%20Lower%20than%20You%20Think.pdf)). Limit orders at the bid or offer roughly halve 0DTE costs ([SEC DERA](https://www.sec.gov/files/dera-hope-reasonable-prc-2503.pdf)).

Commissions are small by comparison. Robinhood charges $0 plus a combined $0.04 regulatory and clearing fee per contract. SPX adds $0.50 per contract plus $0.57–0.66 in exchange fees, while XSP carries no exchange fee below 10 contracts ([Robinhood](https://robinhood.com/us/en/support/articles/trading-fees-on-robinhood)). Fidelity charges $0.65 per contract ([Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/trading/commissions-margin-rates)). That $0.65 is 0.4% of a $1.70 option but 3.25% of a $0.20 lottery ticket.

Retail options trading loses money in aggregate, and spreads are the largest part of the loss. At a 10-day horizon, retail lost **$2.10 billion** between November 2019 and June 2021. Indirect spread costs were about **$6.4 billion**, almost seven times the $887 million in commissions. Losses concentrated in purchases, calls and contracts with less than a week to expiry. The buy portfolio lost even before costs, while retail sellers earned positive but statistically insignificant returns ([BPS 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13285)). Around earnings, retail buyers concentrate in high expected-volatility announcements and lose **5–9% on average and 10–14% around high-volatility events**. They lose by overpaying for implied volatility, crossing wide spreads and holding through the post-announcement volatility crush ([de Silva, So & Smith, Review of Finance 2026](https://academic.oup.com/rof/article/30/2/489/8301159)). In simulations built from observed retail trades held to maturity, more than half of the portfolios lost over 90% of their value ([Ernst & Spatt](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w29883/revisions/w29883.rev0.pdf)). A Cboe-funded counter-study finds Cboe customer trades profitable on average over 2020–2023 ([Amaya et al.](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/education/research_publications/Retail_Profitability.pdf)), so the size of aggregate losses depends on how retail is identified.

## Implications for a retail app that uses daily-bar stock signals (momentum, short-term reversal, distance-from-200-day-trend) to suggest options trades

### What is supported: stock signals pick direction, option data pick the instrument

The evidence supports a narrow role for options. Options can express a direction that comes from a daily-bar stock signal, provided the option layer controls cost and volatility exposure. Five design choices have direct support. First, restrict suggestions to liquid, near-the-money contracts on ETFs and large caps, with tickets large enough to escape the worst spreads. Moving from an under-$250 OTM ticket to a $1,000+ ATM ticket cuts round-trip cost from 11–28% to 2.6–4.2% of premium ([BPS 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13285)). Second, default to limit orders at or near mid ([SEC DERA](https://www.sec.gov/files/dera-hope-reasonable-prc-2503.pdf)). Third, use the gap between implied and realized vol, and the IV term slope, to choose between buying premium and using spreads or short premium. These are the two most robust cross-sectional option signals ([Heston et al.](https://www.acem.sjtu.edu.cn/sffs/2021w/pdfs/3.pdf)). Fourth, avoid long premium into high expected-volatility earnings ([de Silva et al.](https://academic.oup.com/rof/article/30/2/489/8301159)). Fifth, keep any short-volatility suggestion unlevered and defined-risk, given that leverage, not the premium itself, ended LJM, the short-VIX funds and Structured Alpha. Multi-leg retail 0DTE positions also outperformed single-leg ones ([Beckmeyer et al.](https://wp.lancs.ac.uk/fofi2024/files/2024/04/FoFI-2024-146-Leander-Gayda.pdf)).

The momentum and reversal signals the app already computes have one documented, if modest, use in this layer. On a hedged basis, options on recent winners have been relatively cheap and options on recent losers relatively rich ([Zhan et al. WP](https://abfer.org/media/abfer-events-2016/annual-conference/investment-finance/226-option-return-predictability.pdf); [An et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19590.pdf)). That argues for long calls on momentum winners and for bull put spreads rather than long calls on short-term-reversal candidates. Both are hypotheses to test, not established edges.

### What is not supported: leveraged directional buying, naive premium selling, 0DTE timing

Several common product ideas have no support or contrary evidence. "Buy calls on momentum winners, puts on losers" as a standalone edge is unsupported. Long single-stock straddles lose about 5% a month on average, and a directional ATM call needs roughly 0.6–1.0% of monthly stock outperformance just to break even after volatility carry and retail spreads. Distance from the 200-day trend has never been tested on option returns. Systematic index premium selling as a source of alpha has no statistically significant support after about 2012, and on Cboe's own index it has trailed the S&P 500 on a risk-adjusted basis since 2007 ([Dew-Becker & Giglio](https://www.dew-becker.org/documents/option_decline.pdf); [Cboe PUT factsheet](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/indices/factsheet/CboeGlobalIndices_PUT-Index.pdf)). Turning daily-bar signals into 0DTE trades fails after correctly charged costs ([Vilkov log](https://github.com/vilkovgr/0dte-strategies/blob/main/KNOWN-ISSUES.md)). Public end-of-day put-call ratios are not the Pan-Poteshman signal. The smirk no longer predicts at a monthly horizon. Realized-minus-implied vol as a *stock* predictor is reported to have failed. And any bearish signal concentrated in hard-to-borrow names is mostly a borrow-fee proxy that retail cannot harvest ([Muravyev, Pearson & Pollet](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v172y2025ics0304405x25001618.html)).

| 4 | One-month change in put IV minus call IV | 30-day, 0.5-delta surface points | Direction confirmation | 1.68%/month equal-weighted (1996–2011) | Untested after 2011; small-cap tilt |
| 5 | Risk-neutral skewness | Full OTM strike strip, Bakshi-Kapadia-Madan style | Downside-risk filter | t ≈ −11 in every regime 2015–26 (preprint) | One unreviewed source |
| 6 | Option momentum plus one-month option reversal | Past straddle returns rebuilt from daily chains | Rank which contracts to buy or sell | 6.22%/month at mid; 3.48% cost-optimized at 76% of spread (1996–2019) | Dies at full quoted spread; needs 12 months of history |

Ranks 1 and 2 have the strongest replication record and act on the instrument choice the app must make anyway. Ranks 3 to 5 are stock-direction overlays whose recent survival is documented only in an unreviewed preprint, which makes an independent 2020–2026 test especially informative. Rank 6 is the strongest pure option-return signal but survives only with algorithmic execution. Rank 7 directly tests whether the app's existing signals tell it anything about option pricing.

### A backtest the literature would accept

The literature implies a specific test design. On costs:
- Report every result at three cost levels: mid-quote fills (upper bound), about 50% of the quoted spread (typical retail price-improved fills), and the full quoted spread (conservative).
- Add $0–0.65 per contract plus about $0.04 in pass-through fees, and index exchange fees where relevant.
- Apply Heston and coauthors' cost filters: options with spreads under 10% of mid, extreme signal buckets only, and hold to expiry where possible.

On sample handling:
- Report results with and without March 2020.
- Split the period into regimes: the 2020 crash; the 2021 retail peak, when retail's worst month (January 2021) lost $672 million ([BPS 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13285)); 2022, with the bear market and daily SPX expirations from May; and 2023–2026, when the smirk stopped working.
- Lag every signal by one day, so the test does not trade on the same closing quotes used to build the signal.
- Exclude earnings windows for long-premium suggestions.
- Treat stocks with high implied borrow fees separately.

On statistical power:
- 2020–2026 gives about 78 monthly observations. An annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.0 over 6.5 years produces a t-statistic of only about 2.5.
- Goyal and Saretto's finding that only 5 of 29 surviving option signals keep their alpha under a dynamic model is a reminder to hold out 2024–2026 before trusting anything.
- Measure returns per dollar of premium and margin, with expected shortfall and maximum drawdown alongside Sharpe. Cboe itself warns that Sharpe ratios mislead for negatively skewed option payoffs ([Cboe Insights](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/key-cboe-benchmark-indexes-using-spx-options-offer-strong-risk-adjusted-returns/)).

Two conclusions follow. First, the largest and most reliable "edge" in this evidence is cost avoidance, not a signal. The difference between a deep-OTM micro-ticket weekly (about 28% round trip) and a $1,000+ ATM contract (about 3–4%) exceeds every after-cost signal return in the literature. An app that steers users into cheap structures, limit orders and away from earnings IV crush captures value that is documented, rather than hoping for one that is not. Second, the 2020–2026 period is exactly the regime the published research has not covered: after publication of these signals, after the index premium faded, and with record retail participation. The app's own backtests are therefore genuine out-of-sample tests of this literature. A null result is the expected outcome, and a positive one should be treated as a discovery that needs a holdout period, not as confirmation.
