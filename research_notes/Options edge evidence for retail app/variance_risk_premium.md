# Variance Risk Premium (VRP): Implied vs Realized Volatility, Index vs Single-Stock Options, and Short-Vol Tail Risk

Research notes compiled October 2026. Conventions: "VRP" here means risk-neutral (implied) variance or volatility minus subsequently realized variance or volatility. A *positive* implied-minus-realized gap is the same as a *negative* premium on a long-variance position. "Vol points" means annualized volatility in percentage points. Where I could read the primary PDF myself, the numbers below come from that document. Numbers taken from abstracts or secondary summaries are flagged as such.

---

## Q1. How large is the VRP for S&P 500 (SPX/SPY) and Nasdaq-100 (NDX/QQQ) options, in vol points and in strategy returns, and over which sample periods?

### Takeaway
Before about 2012, S&P 500 implied volatility exceeded later realized volatility by about 2.5–5.6 vol points, depending on the implied measure. The gap is about 2.5 points for 30-day ATM implied vol and about 4–5.6 points for the VIX or a variance-swap rate. Selling index variance earned very large average returns: long variance swaps lost more than 50% per month in log terms (1996–2003), and zero-beta ATM straddles lost about 3% per week. The Nasdaq-100 premium was much smaller and not statistically significant in arithmetic terms in the main study that covers it (Carr & Wu, 1996–2003). No credible recent NDX/QQQ-specific estimate was found.

### Cited Findings

**S&P 500 / S&P 100 / Dow, vol-point gaps**
- **Carr & Wu, 1996–2003 (OptionMetrics, Jan 1996–Feb 2003, 30-day horizon).** Average SPX values in vol units: realized 18.82, synthetic variance-swap rate (a model-free, VIX-style measure) 24.41, ATM implied vol 21.28. The variance-swap rate therefore exceeded realized by about 5.6 vol points, and ATM IV exceeded realized by about 2.5 points. OEX: 19.88 realized, 24.61 variance-swap, 22.27 ATM. DJX: 19.67 / 24.71 / 22.50. All three series are positively skewed and fat-tailed. These numbers come from Table 4 of the May 2004 working-paper version hosted by NYU. The published version is Carr & Wu (2009), RFS 22(3):1311–1341, and its exact table values may differ slightly. — [Carr & Wu working paper, May 2004 version (NYU-hosted PDF)](https://engineering.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/2019-01/CarrReviewofFinStudiesMarch2009-a.pdf); [RFS 2009 record (RePEc)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v22y2009i3p1311-1341..html)
- **Carr & Wu, significance.** The mean of 100×(RV − SW) for SPX is −2.279 (t = −7.19). The mean of ln(RV/SW) is −0.594 (t = −9.48). OEX: −1.889 (t = −5.46), log −0.509 (t = −7.83). DJX: −2.039 (t = −5.19), log −0.525 (t = −7.03). The t-stats use Newey-West standard errors with 30 lags. The authors read ln(RV/SW) as the continuously compounded excess return on a long variance swap, and that return was "over −50 percent per month" for the two S&P indexes and the Dow. — [Carr & Wu working paper](https://engineering.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/2019-01/CarrReviewofFinStudiesMarch2009-a.pdf)
- **Carr & Wu, strategy Sharpe.** Shorting S&P/Dow variance swaps gave raw annualized information ratios above 3. Ratios were still above typical stock-portfolio Sharpe ratios after adjusting for serial dependence. The authors warn that Sharpe ratios are misleading for nonlinear payoffs. CAPM beta explains only a small part of the premium, and Fama-French factors explain little. Their conclusion: either the variance-risk market is inefficient, or an independent risk factor is priced heavily. — [Carr & Wu working paper](https://engineering.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/2019-01/CarrReviewofFinStudiesMarch2009-a.pdf)
- **Driessen, Maenhout & Vilkov (DMV), 1996–2012.** For the S&P 500, average annualized realized index vol was 20.69%. Average model-free implied vol (MFIV) was 23.09%, a gap of about 2.4 vol points, and the difference is strongly significant (Newey-West, 21 lags). The point estimate is "very stable across different horizons" (30/60/91-day). — [DMV, "Option-Implied Correlations and the Price of Correlation Risk," Netspar DP 07/2013-061](https://www.netspar.nl/wp-content/uploads/061_Driessen.pdf)
- **DMV, earlier sample (S&P 100, 1996–2003).** A third-party summary of the earlier working paper reports average index implied vol of 24.7% against realized vol of 20.8%, about 3.9 vol points. This is a secondary source summarizing a pre-publication draft. — [CXO Advisory summary](https://cxoadvisory.com/?p=2110); published paper: [DMV (2009), JF 64(3):1377–1406 (RePEc)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v64y2009i3p1377-1406.html)
- **Bondarenko for Cboe, 1990–2018.** Average VIX was 19.3% and average S&P 500 realized vol was 15.1%, a gap of 4.2 vol points. This is a Cboe-sponsored white paper. — [Cboe summary of Bondarenko (May 29, 2019)](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/white-paper-shows-volatility-risk-premium-facilitated-higher-risk-adjusted-returns-for-put-index/)
- **Dew-Becker & Giglio, 1987–2025.** "In the period when there was a large variance risk premium, the VIX's average value was about 3 points above average realized volatility." — [Dew-Becker & Giglio, "The decline of the S&P 500 variance risk premium," June 2, 2026 draft](https://www.dew-becker.org/documents/option_decline.pdf)

**S&P 500, strategy returns**
- **Coval & Shumway (2001, JF), "Expected Option Returns."** Zero-beta ATM S&P index straddles lost about 3% per week on average (an internet appendix elsewhere restates this as −3.15% per week). The authors read this as evidence that systematic stochastic volatility is priced. Sources differ on whether the headline straddle sample is S&P 500 or S&P 100 options; check the original before citing the index. — [Abstract listing (SUFE)](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/361566); [ERIM paper restating ~3%/week](https://www.erim.eur.nl/fileadmin/erim_content/documents/10)
- **Bakshi & Kapadia (2003, RFS 16(2):527–566), "Delta-Hedged Gains and the Negative Market Volatility Risk Premium."** Delta-hedged long S&P 500 index option positions "underperform zero." The underperformance is smaller for options away from the money and larger when volatility is high. The volatility risk premium matters even after accounting for jump fears. These findings are taken from the abstract; I did not access the full text. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v16y2003i2p527-566.html)
- **Bondarenko, "Why Are Put Options So Expensive?" (QJF 2014; working paper 2003/2004).** Using S&P 500 futures options from Aug 1987 to Dec 2000, buying one-month ATM puts returned about −39% per month (≈−40% in Chernov et al.'s citation), and deep-OTM puts about −95% per month. These figures come from the working-paper version, and I did not confirm them in the published QJF text. The paper argues that no model in a broad class can explain the put anomaly. — [CXO summary of 2004 WP](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-options/is-40-per-month-shorting-index-puts-a-fair-return); [Chernov et al. NBER SI 2007 citing −40%/month](https://m.nber.org/conferences/2007/si2007/AP/chernov.pdf); [QJF 2014 (RePEc)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wsi/qjfxxx/v04y2014i03ns2010139214500153.html)
- **Dew-Becker & Giglio, straddle Sharpe ratios.** Over 1987–2001, monthly ATM straddles had a Sharpe ratio of −0.74 for the buyer; Driessen & Maenhout (2007) report −0.21 for a similar sample. After 2001 it was −0.09, and after 2012 it was +0.12. — [Dew-Becker & Giglio (2026 draft)](https://www.dew-becker.org/documents/option_decline.pdf)
- **Heston & Todorov, Jan 2006–Oct 2020.** They use model-free, tradable 28-day option portfolios on futures that replicate realized variance. Average monthly S&P 500 variance-portfolio return was −22.9% excluding March 2020, the second most negative of 20 assets. Including March 2020 it was −11.9%, because the portfolio returned more than 2,000% that month. The monthly standard deviation was about 100% pre-COVID and 192% with COVID, and kurtosis was about 91. Over 2006–2020 as a whole, the realized S&P 500 VRP "was not significantly different from zero." — [Heston & Todorov, "Exploring the Variance Risk Premium Across Assets," AEA 2024 / AFA](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2024/program/paper/hiTeT8SE)
- **AQR illustration (via CAIA).** A delta-hedged S&P 500 put-selling strategy over 1996–2016 earned 1.5% a year with 2.2% volatility, a Sharpe ratio of 0.68. This is a practitioner source and the period includes 2008. — [CAIA / AQR](https://caia.org/node/5509)
- **Frequency.** Implied exceeded realized S&P 500 vol "most of the time, but not always" over 1986–2014, with negative spikes during crises (AQR Exhibit 4 as described by Alpha Architect). A commonly quoted figure of "85% of the time, ~4 points, 1990–2014" could not be traced to a primary source. — [Alpha Architect on AQR](https://alphaarchitect.com/volatility-premium-covered-call-selling-and-knowing-what-you-own/); [AQR, "Understanding the Volatility Risk Premium" (2018)](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/White-Papers/Understanding-the-Volatility-Risk-Premium)

**Nasdaq-100 (NDX / QQQ)**
- **Carr & Wu, 1996–2003.** Average values in vol units for NDX: realized 37.54, variance-swap rate 40.19, ATM IV 38.19. The variance-swap rate exceeded realized by about 2.6 points, and ATM IV by only about 0.65 points. For QQQ: realized 44.96, variance-swap 49.47, ATM 47.07. The arithmetic premium is insignificant for both: NDX 100×(RV−SW) = −1.040 (t = −0.94), QQQ −2.940 (t = −1.82). The log premium is significant: NDX −0.207 (t = −4.42), QQQ −0.257 (t = −3.89). The authors state that the NDX premium is "negative, but with a smaller absolute magnitude" than S&P/Dow. The sample is dominated by the dot-com boom and bust. — [Carr & Wu working paper](https://engineering.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/2019-01/CarrReviewofFinStudiesMarch2009-a.pdf)
- **Nasdaq-sponsored study (Russell Rhoads, Indiana University).** Buyers of 1-day ATM NDX straddles did "much better" than sellers. This is industry-sponsored and summarized secondhand, so I could not verify the sample or method. — [Nasdaq article](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/new-white-paper-explores-ndx-straddle-performance-day-week)

### Inferences
- The size of the "VRP" depends heavily on the implied measure. For SPX in 1996–2003, the variance-swap/VIX-style gap (~5.6 pts) was more than twice the ATM-IV gap (~2.5 pts). A retail app that compares ATM IV with realized vol will show a much smaller edge than the "VIX minus realized" figures often quoted. Part of the VIX gap pays for skew and tail (jump) risk that ATM straddle sellers do not collect.
- The "4.2-point" Cboe figure compares average VIX with an average of short-window realized vols. Averaging square roots of noisy realized variances biases realized vol down (Jensen's inequality), so part of the headline gap is a measurement artifact rather than a harvestable premium. Dew-Becker & Giglio instead compare the VIX with long-window realized standard deviation, and they find the gap is now near zero (see Q4).
- Evidence for a Nasdaq-100 premium is weak, comes from one old and unusual sample, and is not robust in arithmetic (dollar) terms. The ATM-IV-minus-realized gap of about 0.65 points was too small to cover realistic costs.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed, recent (post-2010) estimate of the NDX/QQQ VRP in vol points or short-straddle returns. VXN-vs-realized comparisons found online came from unverifiable blogs and were excluded.
- I could not access the published RFS tables of Carr & Wu or Bakshi & Kapadia's exact delta-hedged gain magnitudes. Carr & Wu figures are from the 2004 working paper.
- Coval & Shumway's exact sample period and index for the straddle result were not verified from the original.
- No credible academic estimate was found for SPY ETF options specifically, as distinct from SPX. They should behave similarly, but this was not verified.

## Q2. Is single-stock implied volatility systematically overpriced the way index vol is? What do Driessen-Maenhout-Vilkov, Bakshi-Kapadia, Carr-Wu and Bollerslev-Tauchen-Zhou find? Is the index premium explained by correlation risk?

### Takeaway
No, not in the same way. In the main studies (Carr-Wu 1996–2003; DMV 1996–2012), average single-stock implied variance is close to realized, and the arithmetic premium is statistically insignificant for most stocks. The large index premium is attributed mainly to priced correlation risk: index options embed implied correlations about 7–10 points above realized. Single-stock options do show *cross-sectional* mispricing: delta-hedged returns are negative and worse for high-idiosyncratic-vol stocks, and IV-vs-HV sorts predict straddle returns. Those are relative-value effects with heavy friction caveats, not a uniform "sell single-stock vol" premium.

### Cited Findings
- **Carr & Wu (2009, RFS), 35 single stocks, 1996–2003.** The mean log premium ln(RV/SW) is significantly negative for 21 of 35 stocks. The arithmetic premium 100×(RV−SW) is "insignificant for all but three of the 35 individual stocks." The authors "conjecture that the market does not price all return variance variation in each single stock, but only prices the variance risk in the stock market portfolio." Cross-sectionally, a stock's log premium is related to its "variance beta" to SPX realized variance (slope 0.2675, t = 2.72, R² = 15.9%). Examples, in vol units (realized / variance-swap / ATM IV): MSFT 38.31 / 42.68 / 39.38; INTC 49.33 / 48.57 / 46.42, where ATM IV was *below* average realized; IBM 36.66 / 39.72 / 36.91. Individual options also have far fewer strikes, mostly single digits against 20+ for SPX, which makes single-stock estimates noisier. — [Carr & Wu working paper (May 2004)](https://engineering.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/2019-01/CarrReviewofFinStudiesMarch2009-a.pdf); [RFS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v22y2009i3p1311-1341..html)
- **Driessen, Maenhout & Vilkov (2009, JF 64(3):1377–1406), "The Price of Correlation Risk: Evidence from Equity Options."** The paper uses S&P 100 index options, options on all index components, and stock returns. It finds priced correlation risk, attributes the index variance risk premium to correlation risk, and reports that the premium "cannot be exploited with realistic trading frictions," which it frames as a limits-to-arbitrage interpretation. A secondary summary of the working paper says individual stock options "do not imply a significant volatility risk premium." — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v64y2009i3p1377-1406.html); [OptionMetrics research listing](https://optionmetrics.com/research/j-driessen-p-maenhout-and-g-vilkov-the-price-of-correlation-risk-evidence-from-equity-options/); [CXO summary](https://cxoadvisory.com/?p=2110)
- **DMV companion paper (1996–2012, S&P 500 and DJ30).** Average implied correlation was 39.46% against realized 32.59% for the S&P 500. For DJ30 the figures were 46.03% and 35.53%. Index options are thus priced as if stock correlations are more than 21% higher (S&P 500) and about 30% higher (DJ30) than realized. Index MFIV averaged 23.09% against realized 20.69%, which is significant. For constituents, the root-mean implied variance was 43.38% against realized 41.99%, *not* statistically different. At a 91-day horizon, average realized variance actually exceeded implied. A zero individual premium is not rejected at 5% for 503 of 919 S&P 500 stocks and for 27 of 43 DJ30 stocks. The authors conclude that "because individual variance risk is not priced on average, the correlation risk premium drives the index variance risk premium." — [DMV Netspar DP 2013](https://www.netspar.nl/wp-content/uploads/061_Driessen.pdf)
- **DMV, subsamples.** The implied-minus-realized correlation gap was largest in 1996–2001 (DJ30: 43.41% implied against 21.35% realized). It shrank in 2002–2007 and grew slightly in 2008–2012, but stayed significant in every subsample. In 2002–2007 there is "some evidence against" a zero individual premium, though zero is still not rejected for about half of stocks. — [DMV Netspar DP 2013](https://www.netspar.nl/wp-content/uploads/061_Driessen.pdf)
- **Bakshi & Kapadia (2003, RFS).** This study uses S&P 500 *index* options only and finds negative delta-hedged gains, which is evidence of a negative market volatility risk premium. The single-stock work by these authors is a separate paper (Bakshi & Kapadia 2003b, J. Derivatives) that I did not retrieve. Later studies using the delta-hedge method on UK (LIFFE) single-stock options found negative delta-hedged gains and a priced idiosyncratic volatility component. Those are thesis-level sources of lower credibility. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v16y2003i2p527-566.html); [NTUST thesis listing](https://etheses.lib.ntust.edu.tw/thesis/detail/afcc1f2682cf5f07358e04e17594b417/?seq=2)
- **Bollerslev, Tauchen & Zhou (2009, RFS 22(11):4463–4492), "Expected Stock Returns and Variance Risk Premia."** This paper concerns the *market* premium (implied minus realized variance) as a *predictor* of equity returns; it does not address single stocks. The VRP explains "a nontrivial fraction" of post-1990 aggregate returns, strongest at the quarterly horizon, where it beats P/E, the default spread and cay. A 2007 draft reports more than 15% R² for quarterly excess returns over 1990–2005, and more than 25% combined with P/E. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=948309); [Federal Reserve FEDS 2007-11](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2007/200711/index.html)
- **Replication flag on BTZ predictability.** Heston & Todorov (2006–2020, 20 futures markets) state: "Contrary to Bollerslev et al. (2009), we find that option-implied variance does not positively predict underlying futures returns for the majority of assets." — [Heston & Todorov](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2024/program/paper/hiTeT8SE)
- **Cao & Han (2013, JFE 108(1):231–249).** Delta-hedged equity option returns decline monotonically as the underlying's idiosyncratic volatility rises. Standard risk factors do not explain this. The authors attribute it to dealers charging more where arbitrage is costlier, and controlling for limits-to-arbitrage proxies weakens the effect by about 40%. A summary of the working paper (about 160,000 ATM positions on more than 5,000 stocks, 1996–2006) reports average delta-hedged calls losing about 0.49% of the stock price by maturity. — [PolyU record](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/cross-section-of-option-returns-and-idiosyncratic-stock-volatilit/); [CXO summary (WP figures)](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=2210)
- **Goyal & Saretto (2009, JFE), "Cross-Section of Option Returns and Volatility."** Sorting stocks on historical-minus-implied vol, a long-short straddle portfolio earns economically and statistically significant monthly returns. One secondhand citation gives 22.7% per month, which I did not verify in the paper. Do, Foster & Gray (2016) reportedly confirm the pattern but question whether it survives transaction costs. — [Abstract listing](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/357238); [EUR thesis citing 22.7%](https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/60787/Master-Thesis.pdf)
- **Recent trend.** Dew-Becker & Giglio cite Gao, He & Hu (2026) as showing that "demand-driven variation in firm-level IVs has declined over time." I did not retrieve that paper. — [Dew-Becker & Giglio (2026 draft), fn. 17](https://www.dew-becker.org/documents/option_decline.pdf)

### Inferences
- Selling single-stock vol has historically harvested little average premium, and it carries idiosyncratic jump risk (earnings, M&A, biotech binaries) that is not diversified within one position. The index premium is mainly a correlation premium. It can be "isolated" by a dispersion trade (short index vol, long constituent vol), but DMV find that frictions prevent exploiting it.
- What looks like a single-stock edge in the literature (Cao-Han, Goyal-Saretto) is a relative-value sort that needs a cross-section of options and is most pronounced in the costliest-to-trade names. It is not a simple "IV > RV" rule.

### Gaps
- Exact published numbers for Bakshi & Kapadia (2003b, J. Derivatives) on individual equity options were not retrieved.
- No credible post-2015 study measuring the average single-stock implied-minus-realized gap was found.
- Goyal-Saretto's 22.7% monthly figure and Do-Foster-Gray's cost critique were not verified against the originals.

## Q3. What are the documented tail losses of short-vol strategies (Oct 1987, 2008, Feb 2018, March 2020), and is crash risk compensated?

### Takeaway
Short-vol returns are strongly negatively skewed, and the worst losses cluster in a handful of days. In February 2018, short-VIX ETPs (XIV) and an options-selling fund (LJM) each lost more than 80–90% in one to two days. In March 2020 a single month cut the 2006–2020 average S&P variance-selling return roughly in half, and an institutional options-selling program (Allianz Structured Alpha) lost billions. The cash-secured ATM put index (PUT) fell 32.7% peak to trough in the GFC, against 50.9% for the S&P 500. Whether crash risk is "fairly" compensated is disputed. Bondarenko argues puts are too expensive for any standard model. Broadie-Chernov-Johannes argue the put returns are statistically consistent with standard models once sampling noise and jump-risk premia are accounted for.

### Cited Findings

**February 2018 ("Volmageddon")**
- On 5 Feb 2018, short-volatility ETPs (XIV, SVXY) suffered "a one-day loss of more than 90%." The mechanism was a feedback loop: falling ETP value raised the notional of their short VIX-futures positions, which forced large VIX-futures purchases at the close, which pushed futures higher and shrank assets further. The paper attributes the episode to "market concentration and hedge and leverage rebalancing." XIV was terminated. — [Augustin, Cheng & Van den Bergen, "Volmageddon and the Failure of Short Volatility Products," FAJ Q3 2021 (CFA Institute summary)](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2021/volmageddon-failure-short-volatility-products); [McGill summary](https://www.mcgill.ca/desautels/channels/news/volmageddon-and-failure-short-volatility-products-331398)
- **LJM Partners.** The firm ran margined short OTM puts and calls on S&P 500 futures and had more than $1 billion AUM in Jan 2018. Its portfolios lost more than 80% on 5–6 Feb 2018 "when the VIX spiked over 20 points," and the firm then closed. The CFTC and SEC brought fraud charges in May 2021 over misstatements of worst-case risk. — [CFTC Release 8392-21](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/8392-21); [SEC Litigation Release 26338](https://www.sec.gov/enforcement-litigation/litigation-releases/lr-26338)
- **BIS view.** The BIS March 2018 Quarterly Review ("Volatility is back") examined the role of volatility-linked ETPs. It concluded that complex and leveraged products can create and amplify market jumps even when the core players are small. — [BIS QR March 2018](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt1803.htm); [BIS press release](https://bis.org/press/p180311.htm)
- **PUT index in 2018.** The PUT index returned −5.9% in calendar 2018, *worse* than the S&P 500 TR's −4.4%. Selling ATM puts did not cushion a year with two volatility shocks. — [Cboe PUT factsheet (as of Aug 31, 2026)](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/indices/factsheet/CboeGlobalIndices_PUT-Index.pdf)

**March 2020**
- The VIX closed at a record 82.69 on 16 March 2020. — [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2020/03/16/wall-streets-fear-gauge-hits-highest-level-ever.html); [Macroption list of VIX highs](https://www.macroption.com/vix-all-time-high/)
- **Heston & Todorov.** The S&P 500 model-free variance portfolio returned more than 2,000% in March 2020. That single month moved the 2006–2020 average monthly S&P VRP from −22.9% to −11.9% and made it statistically insignificant over the full sample. — [Heston & Todorov](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2024/program/paper/hiTeT8SE)
- **Allianz Global Investors "Structured Alpha."** This was an options-trading strategy with about $11 billion invested by about 114 institutional investors. It lost billions after the COVID crash of March 2020 exposed concealed downside risk. One investor risk report had its crash-scenario loss altered from −42.15% to −4.15%. AGI US agreed to pay more than $1 billion to the SEC, and with Allianz SE more than $5 billion in restitution. — [SEC press release 2022-84](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2022-84)
- **PUT index in 2020.** PUT returned +2.1% for the year against +18.4% for the S&P 500 TR, so it recovered less in the V-shaped rebound. — [Cboe PUT factsheet](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/indices/factsheet/CboeGlobalIndices_PUT-Index.pdf)

**2008 and October 1987**
- **GFC.** From Jan 2007, PUT's maximum drawdown was −32.7% against −50.9% for the S&P 500 TR. The VIX reached an intraday high of 89.53 on 24 Oct 2008 and a pre-2020 record close of 80.86 on 20 Nov 2008 (CNBC cites 80.74 on 21 Nov 2008, a minor discrepancy). — [Cboe PUT factsheet](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/indices/factsheet/CboeGlobalIndices_PUT-Index.pdf); [Macroption](https://www.macroption.com/vix-all-time-high/)
- **October 1987.** The VXO (the old VIX methodology) closed at 150.19 on 19 Oct 1987; a Cboe back-estimate puts a VIX-equivalent in the 140s. These are secondary sources. — [Macroption](https://www.macroption.com/vix-all-time-high/); [Schaeffer's](https://www.schaeffersresearch.com/content/analysis/2020/04/01/what-a-historically-high-vix-means-for-stocks)
- **Skewness.** Over 1986–2018, PUT monthly-return skewness was −2.09 against −0.81 for the S&P 500, with beta 0.56 and monthly alpha 0.2% (Cboe-sponsored). — [Cboe / Bondarenko 2019](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/white-paper-shows-volatility-risk-premium-facilitated-higher-risk-adjusted-returns-for-put-index/)
- **Cboe's caveat.** Cboe itself notes that Sharpe ratios assume normality while these indexes are negatively skewed, and its sponsored studies therefore also report Stutzer and Sortino ratios. — [Cboe insights](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/key-cboe-benchmark-indexes-using-spx-options-offer-strong-risk-adjusted-returns/)

**Is crash risk compensated?**
- **Bondarenko (2014, QJF).** Historical S&P 500 put prices are "too high," the "overpriced puts puzzle." ATM puts returned about −39%/month and deep-OTM puts about −95%/month over 1987–2000 (working-paper figures), including the Oct 1987 crash month. No model in the studied class explains it. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wsi/qjfxxx/v04y2014i03ns2010139214500153.html); [CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-options/is-40-per-month-shorting-index-puts-a-fair-return)
- **Counter-evidence: Broadie, Chernov & Johannes (2009, RFS 22(11):4493–4529), "Understanding Index Option Returns."** Large OTM put-writing returns are "not inconsistent," meaning statistically insignificant, relative to Black-Scholes or Heston, because of extreme sampling uncertainty. Straddle and delta-hedged returns, which are less noisy, are explained by jump-risk premia plus estimation risk. — [LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/32229); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=965739)
- **Israelov & Nielsen (2015, FAJ 71(6):44–57), "Covered Calls Uncovered."** In a covered call, the short-volatility exposure realized a Sharpe ratio near 1.0 but contributed only about 10% of the risk. The equity-reversal exposure contributed about 25% of risk for little return. The authors manage option portfolios at AQR. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/ufajxx/v71y2015i6p44-57.html)
- **AQR (Hood, Huss, Israelov, Klein).** Simulations find that fears of short-vol strategies causing a "vicious cycle" crash are "grossly overstated." This is a practitioner view. — [Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/index%2ephp/article/2bswah9z4j2nu66suehvk/portfolio/aqr-risk-paritys-chances-of-exacerbating-a-market-crash-are-grossly-overstated)
- **Cross-market caveat.** A KOSPI 200 index options study did not observe a volatility risk premium, "contrary to those in the S&P 500 index options market." — [Journal of Derivatives and Quantitative Studies](https://www.emerald.com/jdqs/article/17/2/67/205260/The-Volatility-Risk-Premium-in-the-KOSPI200-Index)

### Inferences
- The catastrophic losses in 2018 and 2020 came from *leveraged or margined* short vol: XIV's −1x daily VIX-futures exposure, LJM's margined OTM options, and Allianz's options book. Cash-secured, unlevered ATM put writing (PUT) lost less than the S&P 500 peak to trough, but it still lagged badly in rebounds and in some down years (2018). For a retail app, leverage and position sizing, not the VRP itself, decide whether a short-vol user survives.
- In two of the three regulatory cases (LJM, Allianz), regulators alleged that "worst-case" stress numbers given to investors were understated. Backtested stress figures for short-vol products deserve extra skepticism.
- One month (March 2020) can erase years of harvested premium. Any sample average the app displays is extremely sensitive to whether such months are included.

### Gaps
- I found no primary source giving PUT or BXM index returns specifically for October 1987 or for the single worst months (Oct 2008, Mar 2020). The Bondarenko 2019 paper probably has them, but only Cboe's summary was accessible.
- No primary data on short-straddle P&L for 19 Oct 1987, and no evidence that put writers caused broker failures that month. The searches found only anecdotal, legal-case and secondary material.
- Israelov's "Pathetic Protection" and "Still Not Cheap" papers were not retrieved.

## Q4. Has the VRP shrunk post-publication or in recent years (2015–2025)? What do Cboe strategy benchmark indexes show?

### Takeaway
Yes, the evidence points to substantial decay. Dew-Becker & Giglio (Chicago Fed / Yale, 2025–2026) find a structural break around 2012: after it, standard S&P 500 option-selling strategies have no statistically significant alpha or information ratio, and the VIX has been "nearly an unbiased predictor" of realized vol on a rolling 10-year basis for about 17 years. Heston & Todorov find the 2006–2020 S&P VRP insignificant once March 2020 is included. Since 2007, the Cboe PUT index has a *lower* Sharpe ratio than the S&P 500 (0.52 vs 0.61). Earlier Cboe-sponsored studies (1986–2015/2018) showed PUT beating the S&P on risk-adjusted terms, so the conclusion depends on the sample window.

### Cited Findings
- **Dew-Becker & Giglio, "The decline of the S&P 500 variance risk premium" (June 2, 2026 draft; Chicago Fed WP 2025-17).** They study monthly S&P 500 option returns from 1987:08 to 2025:08 (N = 457): 5%-OTM puts and ATM straddles with and without daily delta hedging, plus variance-swap returns (RV − VIX²)/VIX². They find "a structural break in risk premia around 2012. After 2012… standard options strategies no longer have statistically significant alphas or information ratios." Alphas turn *positive* post-2012 for the straddle, the delta-hedged straddle and the variance-swap series. They stay negative but insignificant for the 95% put and the delta-hedged 95% put. The change is significant at 5% for the straddle and delta-hedged straddle, and at 10% for the delta-hedged put. Betas are stable and volatilities essentially unchanged, so the Sharpe decline comes from means. — [PDF](https://www.dew-becker.org/documents/option_decline.pdf); [Chicago Fed WP 2025-17](https://www.chicagofed.org/publications/working-papers/2025/2025-17)
- **Dew-Becker & Giglio, VIX bias.** With realized vol at 15%, the early rolling-10-year VRP implies an average VIX of 18.7, overstating realized by 25%. The latest window implies 16.4, overstating by only 10%, so the VIX should now average about 2.2 points lower than in the high-VRP era. Comparing rolling 10-year average VIX with rolling 10-year realized standard deviation, the gap "has actually been near zero for 17 years now." — [PDF](https://www.dew-becker.org/documents/option_decline.pdf)
- **Dew-Becker & Giglio, mechanism.** The decay coincides with dealers' net S&P 500 gamma moving from negative to neutral around August 2012, and with lower hedging frictions (underlying bid-ask spreads, basis risk). Their model attributes the decline to retail and other investors becoming able to *supply* options. Their explanation is intermediary-based; the paper says consumption-based crash/disaster explanations are not ruled out. A delta-hedge replication built from volatility forecasts shows no CAPM alpha back to 1926, so the negative-alpha era was roughly 1987 to about 2010–2012. — [PDF](https://www.dew-becker.org/documents/option_decline.pdf)
- **Related work.** Bates (2022) also finds a decline but dates it to about 2017, using weekly hedging and data through 2020. Heston, Jacobs & Kim (2022) find a negative VRP that "cannot be distinguished from simple market (beta) risk." Both are as cited by Dew-Becker & Giglio; I did not read the originals. — [Dew-Becker & Giglio (2026 draft)](https://www.dew-becker.org/documents/option_decline.pdf)
- **Heston & Todorov (Jan 2006–Oct 2020).** "Most assets had significant variance risk premiums, but the realized S&P 500 variance risk premium was not significantly different from zero" over 2006–2020. Including March 2020, the S&P has a smaller negative VRP than most of the 20 assets studied; agricultural commodities and 10-year Treasuries have the most negative. — [Heston & Todorov](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2024/program/paper/hiTeT8SE)
- **DMV, earlier decay signal.** The correlation-risk premium (implied minus realized correlation) was largest in 1996–2001 and smaller in 2002–2007. The authors say this "is suggestive that the correlation risk premium may have become smaller over time," but it remained significant through 2012. — [DMV Netspar DP 2013](https://www.netspar.nl/wp-content/uploads/061_Driessen.pdf)
- **Cboe PUT index factsheet (as of Aug 31, 2026).** Since Jan 3, 2007: PUT returned 7.1% a year with 10.7% volatility, −32.7% maximum drawdown, beta 0.6, Sharpe 0.52 and Sortino 0.69. The S&P 500 TR returned 11.0% with 15.4% volatility, −50.9% drawdown, Sharpe 0.61 and Sortino 0.92. PUT annualized returns were 15.8% (1 year), 12.6% (3 years), 9.2% (5 years) and 8.4% (10 years). Calendar years, PUT vs S&P 500 TR: 2015 6.4/1.4; 2016 7.8/12.0; 2017 10.8/21.8; 2018 −5.9/−4.4; 2019 13.5/31.5; 2020 2.1/18.4; 2021 21.8/28.7; 2022 −7.7/−18.1; 2023 14.3/26.3; 2024 17.8/25.0; 2025 9.2/17.9. Compounding these gives about 7.8% a year for PUT over 2015–2025 against about 13.5% for the S&P 500 TR (my calculation). PUT beat the S&P only in 2015 and 2022. — [Cboe PUT factsheet](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/indices/factsheet/CboeGlobalIndices_PUT-Index.pdf)
- **Earlier Cboe-sponsored results (Bondarenko, Jan 2016).** From Jul 1986 to Dec 2015, PUT returned 10.1% a year at 10.1% vol (Sharpe 0.67) against the S&P 500 TR's 9.8% at 15.3% (Sharpe 0.47). From Feb 2006 to Dec 2015: PUT 6.6% at 11.5% (Sharpe 0.52, max drawdown −33%); weekly WPUT 5.6% at 9.8% (Sharpe 0.50, max drawdown −24%); S&P 500 TR 7.1% at 15.1% (Sharpe 0.46, max drawdown −51%). All figures are gross of costs, and pre-launch history is back-filled. — [CXO Advisory summary of Bondarenko (2016)](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-options/performance-of-cboe-putwrite-indexes); [Cboe IR release](https://ir.cboe.com/news/news-details/2016/New-Study-On-Weekly-Monthly-SP-500-PutWrite-Indexes-Released-01-27-2016/default.aspx)
- **Bondarenko (May 2019, Cboe-sponsored), Jun 1986–Dec 2018.** PUT, CMBO, BXMD and BXM all had higher Sharpe and Sortino ratios than the S&P 500. PUT rose 1,835% cumulatively with 9.95% volatility against 14.93% for the S&P. — [Cboe summary](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/white-paper-shows-volatility-risk-premium-facilitated-higher-risk-adjusted-returns-for-put-index/); [Cboe insights](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/key-cboe-benchmark-indexes-using-spx-options-offer-strong-risk-adjusted-returns/)
- **Covered-call ETPs.** Exchange-traded products tracking BXM "substantially underperform" the index itself, according to the same CXO summary; no figures were given. — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-options/performance-of-cboe-putwrite-indexes)

### Inferences
- The strongest "sell index vol" evidence comes from samples that end before about 2012 or that include 1986–2000s data. Results from 2012 onward are much weaker and possibly zero on an alpha basis. Since 2007, PUT behaves like a lower-beta equity exposure (beta about 0.6, Sharpe below the index) rather than a source of alpha. The pattern fits post-publication decay plus structural change: more option supply from retail, ETFs and overwriting funds, and lower dealer frictions.
- A raw VIX-minus-realized gap is still usually positive day to day, but long-horizon averages that include crash months now show a small gap. A retail app should not present "IV > RV" as evidence of a free edge.
- The PUT index is mostly ATM put-selling, which captures equity beta plus a VRP component. Its post-2007 lag largely reflects a strong equity bull market in which capped upside hurts. Dew-Becker & Giglio's delta-hedged and CAPM-alpha results control for that and still find no post-2012 premium.

### Gaps
- Dew-Becker & Giglio is a working paper (latest draft June 2026), not yet confirmed as peer-reviewed. Bates (2022) dates the break differently (2017). The exact break date is therefore uncertain.
- No calendar-year BXM, BXMD or CMBO factsheet numbers for 2015–2025 were retrieved.
- No credible peer-reviewed evidence was found on 0DTE (same-day) SPX option premia. Only secondary or blog summaries surfaced, for example a claimed "Frankfurt School" study finding a near-zero intraday VRP and a University of Münster estimate of retail 0DTE losses reported by Bloomberg (paywalled), and these were not verifiable.
- No study isolating 2015–2025 for single-stock VRP was found.

## Q5. How does the VIX relate to at-the-money implied vol, and how large is the gap?

### Takeaway
The VIX is a model-free variance-swap rate built from a 1/K²-weighted strip of OTM options, so with a downward-sloping SPX skew it sits above 30-day ATM implied vol. A practitioner rule of thumb, published on Cboe's site, is VIX ≈ 1.1–1.2 × ATM vol. Academic data agree: in Carr & Wu (SPX, 1996–2003) the synthetic variance-swap rate averaged 24.41 against 21.28 for ATM IV, about 3.1 vol points or a ratio of about 1.15. A sizable share of the "VIX minus realized" premium is therefore a skew and convexity premium, not something an ATM straddle seller earns.

### Cited Findings
- "Typically, the VIX Index is around 1.1-1.2 times ATMV." Lower strikes are weighted more heavily (by 1/K²) in the variance calculation, so put skew lifts the VIX above ATM vol. With zero skew, VIX equals ATMV, and the spread widens as skew rises. In a worked example, ATMV of 25% with a skew parameter of 1.5 gives a VIX of 27.9%, about 2.9 points or 1.12×. The spread can compress: during the 2022 sell-off, skew fell from all-time highs to multi-year lows and the VIX-ATMV spread narrowed. Source: guest article by Carmika Partners (Cameron, Vestergaard, Mudan) on Cboe Insights, Oct 28, 2022. — [Cboe Insights](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/how-can-long-volatility-go-down-when-the-vix-index-goes-up)
- **Carr & Wu, 1996–2003, 30-day.** Synthetic variance-swap rate (≈ VIX² methodology) vs ATM implied vol (their volatility-swap proxy) vs realized: SPX 24.41 / 21.28 / 18.82; OEX 24.61 / 22.27 / 19.88; DJX 24.71 / 22.50 / 19.67; NDX 40.19 / 38.19 / 37.54. The variance-swap rate exceeds the squared ATM/vol-swap rate by Jensen's inequality, and the difference measures the risk-neutral variance of volatility. The authors find the premium is more negative when that vol-of-vol is high. — [Carr & Wu working paper](https://engineering.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/2019-01/CarrReviewofFinStudiesMarch2009-a.pdf)
- Carr & Wu and Dew-Becker & Giglio both note that under an approximately geometric-diffusion S&P 500, the VIX is the square root of the variance-swap price. — [Dew-Becker & Giglio (2026 draft)](https://www.dew-becker.org/documents/option_decline.pdf)

### Inferences
- Rough decomposition for SPX in 1996–2003: VIX-style (24.4) minus realized (18.8) ≈ 5.6 points. About 3.1 points is "VIX over ATM", reflecting skew and vol-of-vol convexity, and about 2.5 points is "ATM over realized." An app that quotes "VIX minus realized" as the edge available to ATM option sellers would overstate it by roughly half or more.
- The VIX/ATM ratio is not constant. It narrows when skew flattens, as in 2022, and the VIX can then rise less than ATM vol in a sell-off. This matters for any app signal that uses the VIX as a stand-in for ATM IV.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed long-run time series (for example 2010–2025) of the VIX-minus-ATM spread in vol points. The 1.1–1.2× ratio is a practitioner rule of thumb published on Cboe's site, not a Cboe-authored statistical study.
- Carr & Wu's ATM-vs-variance-swap figures cover only 1996–2003 and come from a working-paper version.
